Sewer backup: what to do before the jetter arrives

  • Stop running water, including washers and dishwashers.
  • Flush no toilets until the line is cleared.
  • Move belongings off the floor near the lowest drain in the house.
  • Keep children and pets away from standing wastewater and wash hands after any contact.
  • Find your cleanout if you can. A cap near the foundation or in a side yard helps the crew start faster.

Marina Coast Water District asks anyone with a sewer backup or spill to call (831) 384-6131 or (831) 883-5900. If wastewater is flowing in the street or yard, that call matters even after we arrive.

What hydro jetting does that a cable cannot

A drain cable pokes a hole through a blockage. A hydro jetter feeds a hose with a specially shaped nozzle into the line, then sends pressurized water out backward-facing jets that pull the hose forward and scour the whole pipe wall. The debris flushes downstream rather than being pushed aside.

That makes jetting the better tool for thick grease, layered scale and fine roots. It is also useful when the same line backs up every few months, since the cause is usually buildup on the walls and not a single plug. For a simple one-fixture clog, drain cleaning with a cable is quicker and costs less.

When we recommend against jetting

Honesty matters here, because jetting a weak pipe can turn a clog into a collapse. We would rather walk away from the jet than damage your line.

  • Old, corroded cast iron can be fragile. Trade guidance says high pressure can worsen corrosion or structural weakness, and recommends a camera look first.
  • Orangeburg, the tar-and-fiber pipe used decades ago, deforms with age. Trade press notes that a flattened pipe can be damaged further by drain-cleaning tools, so we avoid aggressive jetting on it.
  • Clay pipe with cracks or offset joints can shift when water hits the break.
  • A line already collapsed or broken needs repair, not cleaning. See sewer line repair.

Trade sources also set limits by material. One manufacturer says to keep pressure at the tool at or below 1,400 psi for plastic gravity sewer pipe, and a liner maker says 2,000 psi at the nozzle for lined pipe. We choose the pressure and nozzle for the pipe we see on camera, not a default setting.

Grease, roots and the usual Marina culprits

Grease is the classic reason to jet. Marina Coast Water District says fats, oils and grease reform and solidify in pipes even after hot water and soap, so a line that handles a lot of cooking waste builds a waxy lining. Jetting is how that lining comes off.

Roots are the other repeat offender. Fine roots find small gaps in pipe joints and thicken into a mat. A jet with a root-cutting nozzle can clear the mat, but roots grow back if the joint stays open. If a camera shows an open joint, repair is the long-term answer.

Under district code the owner maintains the side sewer, the pipe between the house and the district main. The district also encourages owners to keep that lateral clear of trash and grease, so a routine cleaning can be cheaper than an emergency call. For businesses, the district's grease program requires interceptors to be pumped at least every three months, with records kept three years. Our commercial plumbing page covers business service.

How a jetting visit runs

  1. Assess

    We ask about the pipe age and material, check for a cleanout and look at how the backup behaves.

  2. Inspect when needed

    A camera run shows whether the pipe can take pressure. See sewer inspection for what that involves.

  3. Jet

    We push the nozzle through, work back toward the cleanout and pull debris out as we go.

  4. Confirm

    We run water to check the flow, then explain what we found and what, if anything, comes next.
