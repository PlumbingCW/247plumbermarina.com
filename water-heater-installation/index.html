Planning your water heater installation

Good installs start with a few facts. We look at where the heater sits now, what fuel feeds it, how many people use hot water, and whether the vent, gas line or circuit can support a different unit.

  • Fuel at the house: natural gas, propane or electric only.
  • Tank label: capacity, age and model number.
  • Location: garage, closet, attic, utility room or outdoors.
  • Household size and how many baths run at once.
  • Any plan to move the heater, such as a remodel or a garage conversion.

Moving a heater costs more than swapping in place, because gas, water, vent and drain connections all move. If you are considering a new location, tell us early.

Safety steps before any heater work

A gas smell means you leave first. Get outside, phone 911 from a distance, then reach PG&E at 1-800-743-5000.

  • Close the cold water inlet if a tank is leaking.
  • Switch off the heater's breaker only if you can reach the panel safely and the floor is dry.
  • Keep children and pets back, since water from a failing heater can be hot enough to scald.

Sizing the heater for your household

A tank that is too small runs out of hot water on busy mornings. One that is too large wastes energy keeping extra water warm. Tank capacity usually follows the number of people in the home and the number of bathrooms.

Tankless units are sized by flow rate in gallons per minute and by temperature rise, so the number of fixtures running together matters more than household size. Large families with two showers at once often need a larger unit, or two.

Bigger tanks cost more. A 75-gallon heater typically runs $1,500 to $3,400 installed in national guides, with hybrid models higher. Match size to real use rather than buying the largest option.

Code details that affect the install

According to the California Plumbing Code and the state Health and Safety Code, water heaters carry specific safety and anchoring requirements. These are the items we plan for on every install.

Seismic bracingStraps the tank so it cannot tip in an earthquakeRequired by Health and Safety Code 19211 for replacement units, and the California Plumbing Code points to the same rule

Strapping typically means two straps, one in the upper third and one in the lower third of the tank, with the lower strap at least 4 inches above the controls. That placement keeps the tank steady without blocking the gas controls.

We do not state a Marina permit rule without checking it. The Building Division can tell you what applies to your project, and heat pump or fuel-switch questions are best settled with them before purchase.

Where the heater goes: garage, closet or attic

Location drives labor. A garage install is usually the most direct. A closet needs clearance for combustion air and the vent. An attic needs a pan and drain line so a failure does not soak the ceiling below, and national guides show attic installs adding roughly $200 to $1,000.

A drain pan with a line to a safe spot is worth adding anywhere a leak could reach finished floors. Water heaters fail eventually, and a pan limits what happens when yours does.

Marina water and your new heater

MCWD's 2025 water quality report puts average hardness near 10.5 grains per gallon, within a range of 4.9 to 19.3. The water is chlorine-disinfected. Hard water leaves scale on heating surfaces, so a new heater benefits from regular flushing, as the district recommends.
