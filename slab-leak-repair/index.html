Slab leak emergency: the first 15 minutes

Water that has nowhere to go spreads under flooring and into walls. Act quickly, even if the leak looks small.

  1. Close the main valve

    Turn off the water at the house shutoff. Then open one faucet to relieve pressure and see whether the sound stops.

  2. Protect electrics

    Do not touch outlets or cords on wet floors. Switch off the breaker for a flooded room only if the panel is dry and reachable.

  3. Lift what you can

    Pull up rugs and move furniture. Wet carpet pad and wood flooring are hard to save after a day or two.

  4. Check the meter

    With the main closed, a moving meter dial points to a leak before the house valve. Call the district's 24-hour line at (831) 883-5900 if the meter box is wet.

  5. Call us

    Phone (855) 575-2890 any hour, including nights and weekends. Tell us what you hear, see and feel underfoot.

Gas smell?

If you smell rotten eggs or hear hissing, leave the building, call 911 from a safe distance, then call PG&E at 1-800-743-5000. A slab leak is never a reason to stay inside.

How slab leaks show up

People rarely see a slab leak. They notice effects: a hot spot on the floor, the sound of water at night, or a bill that no longer matches how they live. Some find damp baseboards, curling laminate or a musty smell along one wall.

  • A warm or hot patch on the floor, which can mean a hot water line is leaking beneath it.
  • Running water sounds with every fixture off.
  • Cracks, lifted tile or floors that feel soft.
  • Low hot water pressure, or a water heater that seems to run constantly.
  • A meter that keeps moving when the house is quiet.

These signs overlap with other problems, so a leak should be confirmed before concrete is touched. Our leak detection service covers the confirming step.

Spot repair, tunnel, lining or reroute

There is no single best fix. The right choice depends on how many pipes are failing, where they run, and how much disruption you can accept.

OptionWhat happensTrade-off
Break the slab and spot repairConcrete is opened above the leak and the pipe section is replacedDirect fix, but flooring and concrete need patching
Tunnel under the slabAccess is dug from outside to reach the pipe from belowKeeps floors intact, but needs outdoor digging
Pipe liningA coating is applied inside the existing pipeLess demolition, though not suited to every pipe
RerouteOld under-slab lines are capped and new ones run overhead or in wallsHigher cost, and often best when several pipes are failing

A spot repair answers one leak. If a second or third pinhole follows, rerouting can be cheaper than repeated visits. For a whole house of aging lines, see repiping.

Sandy soil and the ground under Marina slabs

A state environmental document for a project in the Marina area describes extensive sand dunes made of unconsolidated, highly erodible sand. It also notes a relatively high potential for liquefaction in loose, sandy soils within 50 feet of the surface. That is regional geology, not a finding about any one lot.

What it means for you: ground support can differ from one lot to the next, so a plumber should look at the whole slab layout and not only the wet spot. In general, soil that settles or shifts can stress pipes that are embedded in or under concrete. That is general plumbing knowledge, and no one can say it caused your leak without inspecting the pipe.

Outdoor digging in loose sand also needs care, because trench walls can slump. Tunnel and outside-access repairs are planned with that in mind.

Insurance, tenants and the 72-hour rule

According to the California Department of Insurance, homeowners policies typically cover sudden and accidental water damage, but not seepage, leaks, wear and tear, or neglect. One cost guide says slab leaks are usually not covered, though policies vary. Read your exclusions and call your insurer before repairs begin. Photos and a written leak report help. This is not coverage advice.

Under Marina Coast Water District rules, plumbing leaks must be repaired within 72 hours, and the owner maintains the pipes on the customer side of the meter.
